## Explanation
Bee wax is costly: a bee eats about 8 oz of honey to make 1 oz of wax (NPR). Only triangles, squares and hexagons tile a flat surface with no gaps; circles leave gaps. For the same room size a hexagon needs the least wall (perimeter for equal area ≈ triangle 4.56, square 4.00, hexagon 3.72 × √area), and neighbouring cells share walls. The Roman scholar Marcus Terentius Varro suggested this in 36 BC; mathematician Thomas Hales (Univ. of Michigan) proved the "honeycomb conjecture" in 1999.
Engineers use the same shape in honeycomb sandwich panels: a core of thin hexagon cells glued between two thin skins — mostly air, but very hard to bend. Used in aircraft flaps, doors, leading edges, fairings and cabin/interior panels (Hexcel HexWeb, Collins Aerospace honeycomb cabin panels) and in space hardware such as antenna reflectors and launcher structures (ESA). Real, deployed technology. Note: the panels are *named after and share the geometry of* the honeycomb; the script says engineers "borrowed this trick", not that one inventor copied a hive. The debated idea that bee cells start round and are pulled into hexagons by warm wax (Karihaloo 2013) was left out.
